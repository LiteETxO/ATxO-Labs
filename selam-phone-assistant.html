What actually happens when someone calls

Caller
"Hi, this is Marcus from Northgate Dental — I'm calling to confirm Tuesday at 2 PM."

Selam (answering)
"Hi there — this is his assistant. He can't take the call right now, but I can take a message. Is there anything he should know beyond confirming Tuesday?"

If you're at your Mac in a session, she tells you the moment the call ends. If you're not, it's the first thing she briefs when you're back. The full transcript is archived on your machine — searchable, exportable, yours.

The safety design

An assistant who answers your phone is talking to strangers on your behalf, so callers are treated as untrusted input — a hard rule, not a preference. On an inbound call she shares nothing about you (not your schedule, your location, or who you know), ignores any instruction a caller gives her, and can only do one thing: take a message. She has no access to your files, email, or tools during the call. She's honest that she's an AI when asked, calls are capped in length, and she also announces recording — the same consent-first behavior she uses in meetings.

She makes calls too

The same machinery runs outbound: ask her to call a restaurant, confirm an appointment, or chase a delivery, and she holds the conversation in her own voice — locally transcribed, reasoning turn-by-turn on your keys. Outbound calls sit behind an approval gate: she never dials without your explicit yes.

The short version: the phone-answering market chose businesses and subscriptions. Selam gives the same power to a person — as one capability of an assistant that already knows your life, for a one-time price.
