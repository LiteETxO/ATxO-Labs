Selam vs. OpenWorker — same architecture, different species.

OpenWorker is Andrew Ng's open-source desktop AI coworker: free, MIT-licensed, local-first, bring-your-own-keys, MCP-native. We think it's excellent — partly because it validates every architectural bet we made. If you're deciding between them, here's the honest version.

Where we agree completely

Both projects reject the same thing: agents that execute in someone else's cloud, on a metered subscription, with your files streamed to their servers. OpenWorker and Selam both run on your machine, both use your own model keys (Anthropic, OpenAI, Gemini — or local models), both speak MCP to connect tools, and both ship finished work instead of chat. If that architecture matters to you, you're choosing between two implementations of the same principle — not two philosophies.

The honest part

If you're a developer who lives in a terminal, doesn't care about voice or embodiment, and wants a free deliverable engine — use OpenWorker. It's good, it's free, and Andrew Ng's team will keep making it better. We mean that.

Selam is for a different person: someone who wants to talk to their assistant while making coffee, have her sit quietly in a meeting and brief them after, answer the phone when they can't, notice when they walk away mid-presentation, and keep every bit of what she knows in plain files on their Mac. OpenWorker has no face, no voice, no phone, no camera, and no ambition to be a presence in your day — by design. That's not a criticism; it's a different species.

The short version: OpenWorker proved the architecture is right. Selam is what happens when that architecture grows a face, a voice, a phone number, and a personality — shipped as a finished product instead of a repository.
